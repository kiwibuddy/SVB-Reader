## 1 · The product in one page

SourceView Together is a Bible you read out loud, by voice.

Every word of Scripture in the app is attributed to whoever said it. The narrator, divine speech, the
named people at the centre of each story, and everyone else each read in their own **source colour**,
with the speaker's name sitting alongside their words. The text is laid out as **365 complete stories**
with real beginnings and endings, not chapters — one a day for a year, or whenever you like.

Because every voice is already marked, several people can open the same story, each take a colour, and
read that voice aloud. No setup, no host, no assigning parts. When the story ends, **Talk about it**
offers four discussion questions written three ways: for a family, a classroom, or a small group.

It is free, has no ads and no subscription, requires no account, works offline, and keeps everything you
do — progress, reactions, notes — in a database on the device.

**The numbers, verified against the shipping data:**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Stories | 365, plus 66 book introductions |
| Attributed words | 747,039 across the 365 stories |
| Distinct voices | 773 — 4 narration, 5 divine, 90 principals, 674 supporting |
| Average story | 2,047 words · about 10 minutes read silently, nearer 15 read aloud |
| Story length range | 2 to 14 minutes; over half fall between 8 and 12 |
| Divisions | 10, Genesis to Revelation, on one screen when collapsed |
| Plans and challenges | 12 built in, plus unlimited custom plans |
| Discussion questions | 4 per story × 3 audiences, in English and French — over 4,300 per language, plus a second set for family and school |
| Languages | English and French, questions included |
| Price | Free. No ads, no subscription, no account |

## 2 · Why the product exists

### The problem, stated plainly

Most people who want to read the Bible do not stop because they lack an app. They stop because of three
specific frictions, and this product was built against all three.

1. **They lose the thread of who is speaking.** Scripture is dense with dialogue, quotation inside
   quotation, and speakers introduced dozens of verses earlier. Comprehension collapses quietly, and the
   reader concludes they are bad at this.
2. **They read fragments and never finish anything.** A chapter is a medieval typesetting convenience, not
   a unit of meaning. Reading "Genesis 1" ends mid-thought. Nothing gets completed, so nothing gets
   remembered.
3. **They read alone when they wanted to read with people.** Family devotions and small groups collapse
   into one person reading aloud while everyone else waits, or into a leader carrying the whole hour.

### The three convictions

The product is an argument, made in software, that Scripture is better:

- **read together** — it was a communal text long before it was a private one;
- **read out loud** — hearing several voices in a room does something silent reading does not;
- **read whole** — a story with a beginning and an end teaches what a fragment cannot.

Every design decision in section 6 traces back to one of these three. When a feature request doesn't serve
one of them, that is the reason to decline it.

## 3 · Brand platform

**Purpose.** To let people hear Scripture as the conversation it is, and to make reading it together the
easiest thing in the room.

**Proposition.** *Every word in the Bible was said by someone. This app never lets you forget who.*

**Master line.** A Bible you read out loud, by voice.

**Support line.** 773 voices. 365 stories. Four source colours. About fifteen minutes.

**Brand pillars** — every asset should be traceable to one:

| Pillar | What it means | The feeling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Attributed** | Every word belongs to someone, and we show you who | Clarity, relief — "oh, *that's* who's speaking" |
| **Whole** | Stories with beginnings and endings, not fragments | Completion, satisfaction |
| **Aloud** | Built to be spoken in a room with other people | Warmth, participation, a little bit of theatre |
| **Honest** | Free, offline, private, no numbers we can't cite | Trust, no catch |

**Personality.** Quiet and exact. We are the design studio in the room, not the youth pastor with a
microphone. Short sentences. Real detail over adjectives. Never breathless, never salesy, never
guilt-trippy about how much someone reads. Humour is dry and rare. Reverence is shown through care and
craft, not through solemn language.

**Tone by surface.** Store copy: plain and factual. Social: short, visual, confident. Partnership decks
(schools, churches): concrete and practical — what happens in a room, at what time, with what prep.
In-app: fewer words than you think.

## 4 · Audiences and user stories

Five segments, ranked by fit. Each carries proper user stories — these are the stories the app should be
sold on, because they are the ones the app actually delivers.

---

### A · Families at the table  *(primary)*

**Who.** A parent who wants a shared spiritual practice at home and has tried and abandoned two or three.
Kids from about six to sixteen. Fifteen minutes on a weeknight, at most, usually after a meal.

**The job to be done.** "Give us something to do together at the table that isn't a lecture from me and
isn't a screen each."

**User stories**

- As a parent, I want everyone at the table to have a part within seconds, **so that** family Bible time is
  a shared thing rather than me reading at my children.
- As a parent, I want to know how long tonight's story is before we start, **so that** I can promise fifteen
  minutes and keep the promise. *(Every story shows its reading time on the thread.)*
- As a nine-year-old, I want a colour that is mine and lines that are clearly mine, **so that** I know when to
  speak and never have to be told to catch up.
- As a parent, I want the questions afterwards to already exist, **so that** I don't have to invent
  discussion or prepare anything.
- As a parent, I want to pick up where we left off without navigating, **so that** the evening starts with
  reading rather than with me poking at an app.
- As a teenager, I want to react to a line with an emoji instead of announcing a feeling to my family,
  **so that** I can engage without performing.

**The scene we sell.** Four phones around a table. Dad reads the narrator, the eldest takes the principal
green, the six-year-old gets blue and is watching for her turn like a hawk. Twelve minutes. Then three
questions off the screen, and someone actually answers the second one properly.

**Objection and answer.** *"My kids won't read aloud."* — They will read four lines in a colour that is
theirs. It's a part in a play, not a performance.

**Lead features.** Source colours · reading times on the thread · Talk about it (family set) · reactions.

**Ask.** Open tonight's story. Hand out the colours. Read one story out loud.

---

### B · Small groups and home churches

**Who.** A volunteer leader, not a paid pastor, hosting six to twelve adults fortnightly. Prep time is the
scarcest resource in their week.

**The job to be done.** "Fill ninety minutes with something better than me talking, without spending
Saturday preparing it."

**User stories**

- As a group leader, I want the reading to involve everyone in the room, **so that** discussion doesn't
  depend on the same three extroverts.
- As a group leader, I want to read a whole story rather than six verses, **so that** we discuss what the
  passage actually says instead of what a fragment implies.
- As a group leader, I want discussion questions written for adults in a group, **so that** I arrive with
  nothing prepared and it still works.
- As a group member, I want to save the exact thing that struck me, with the speaker attached, **so that**
  I can find it again on Thursday. *(Reactions and notes attach to a whole turn of speech, not a verse
  fragment.)*
- As a group leader, I want the group to be on the same plan without accounts or invitations, **so that**
  nobody is locked out by a login.

**The scene we sell.** Six adults, six phones, one story from Acts. The quiet member has blue and speaks
more in twenty minutes than in the last three meetings.

**Objection and answer.** *"We already have a study guide."* — Keep it. This is the reading, not the study.
It takes twenty minutes and hands you the room already talking.

**Lead features.** Take a colour · whole stories · Talk about it (small-group set) · saved reactions and
notes · plans.

**Ask.** Same story, phones out, take a colour, twenty minutes.

---

### C · Schools, chapel and youth groups

**Who.** A chaplain, RE teacher or youth pastor with a fixed period, a mixed-belief room, and teenagers who
will not volunteer to read.

**The job to be done.** "Get thirty teenagers into a biblical text in one period without it being awkward
or babyish."

**User stories**

- As a chaplain, I want a Bible reading that fits one period, **so that** I can run it weekly without
  restructuring the timetable. *(Most stories run 8–12 minutes; questions add five.)*
- As a teacher, I want a plan sized to an academic year, **so that** the year has a spine. *(Bible in 1
  School Year — 180 stories.)*
- As a student, I want to read a part rather than "share", **so that** speaking in the room carries no
  social risk.
- As a teacher, I want questions written for a classroom rather than a pulpit, **so that** the discussion
  suits a room where not everyone believes this.
- As a chaplain, I want it to work on school wifi or none at all, **so that** the lesson doesn't depend on
  the network.
- As a school, we want no accounts, no data collection and no cost, **so that** it clears a safeguarding
  and procurement review without a process.

**The scene we sell.** Four students at the front, four colours, one story from Acts read cold. The bell
goes before the questions run out.

**Objection and answer.** *"Our students find the Bible impenetrable."* — That is precisely the friction
this removes. They can see who's talking, and the story ends.

**Lead features.** Bible in 1 School Year · school question set · take a colour · free, no accounts,
offline · French for bilingual and immersion schools.

**Ask.** One story per chapel or form time. Rotate colours weekly.

---

### D · The lapsed one-year-plan reader

**Who.** Has started a read-the-Bible-in-a-year plan at least twice. Quit both times, once in Leviticus,
once in March. Carries low-grade guilt about it.

**The job to be done.** "Let me get back into the Bible without signing up for a year of failure."

**User stories**

- As a returning reader, I want to finish one complete thing today, **so that** I get the satisfaction of
  completion instead of a quota.
- As a returning reader, I want to pause a plan and resume it without losing my place or being shamed,
  **so that** a busy fortnight doesn't end the practice.
- As a reader, I want to see where I am in the whole Bible at a glance, **so that** progress feels like a
  journey rather than a checklist. *(The thread.)*
- As a reader, I want a streak that follows my own calendar day, **so that** it is honest and I trust it.
- As a reader, I want to be told what I've been skipping, **so that** I read the parts I avoid.
  *(The You tab's thin-eras insight.)*
- As a reader, I want to build a plan out of exactly what I care about, **so that** my reading follows my
  question rather than a catalogue. *(Custom plans — pick stories, whole books, or every story one voice
  speaks in.)*

**The scene we sell.** Someone who hasn't opened a Bible since March reads *God Creates* in six minutes,
and the app says: one story, one voice met, done. No year attached.

**Objection and answer.** *"I'll quit again."* — Possibly. The unit here is one story, and it takes ten
minutes. There's less to quit.

**Lead features.** Whole stories · the thread · pause/resume · streak on the local calendar · You tab
insights · custom plans.

**Ask.** Start with one story. Not the year.

---

### E · Curious and returning readers

**Who.** Culturally curious, possibly not a believer, possibly returning after years away. Has opened a
Bible, found it confusing, and closed it.

**The job to be done.** "Let me find out what's actually in this book without joining anything."

**User stories**

- As a curious reader, I want to know who is speaking without cross-referencing, **so that** I can follow the
  text on its own terms.
- As a curious reader, I want to read about a specific person, **so that** I can follow a thread I'm
  actually interested in. *(Search by voice; a custom plan of every story they speak in.)*
- As a curious reader, I want no account and no email, **so that** reading this commits me to nothing.
- As a curious reader, I want to see who a person actually talked to and their longest exchange, **so that**
  the book reads like history involving people rather than a religious artefact. *(Cast: Abraham's longest
  back-and-forth is 8 turns, with God, in Sodom & Gomorrah.)*

**The scene we sell.** Someone taps Jesus in Cast and finds 41,239 words across 47 stories, most spoken to
the disciples, the crowd, and the people arguing with him — and taps straight into the argument.

**Lead features.** Attribution · Cast and the conversation graph · search · no account · free.

**Ask.** Read the first story. That's the whole ask.

## 5 · Features, mechanisms, and the impact each is built to create

Read this as a chain: the feature is what we built, the mechanism is *why it works on a person*, and the
impact is what changes for them. Marketing copy should sell the impact and show the feature.

| Feature | Mechanism | Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source colours** — four colours, speaker names beside the words | Removes the working-memory cost of tracking speakers; dialogue is parsed pre-attentively, by colour, before reading begins | Comprehension without effort. The reader stops concluding they're bad at this |
| **Speech-bubble layout** — divine voices and narration on one side, humanity on the other | Turns text into a conversation with two sides, which is how people already read messages | Scripture reads as dialogue between parties, not as a monologue of instruction |
| **365 stories** — natural arcs, each 2–14 minutes | A unit with an ending produces closure; closure produces return | Something is finished every day. Memory attaches to complete stories, not fragments |
| **The thread** — one continuous path through ten divisions | Spatial memory: progress has a shape and a location instead of a count | The year feels walkable. Where you are is a place, not a percentage |
| **Take a colour** — group reading with no setup | Coordination moved out of software and into the page: the colours *are* the assignment | Group reading starts in ten seconds. The feature nobody has to configure is the feature people actually use |
| **Talk about it** — 4 questions, 3 audiences, both languages | Removes the leader's preparation burden at the exact moment a group falls silent | Discussion happens by default. Prep drops to zero |
| **Cast** — 773 voices with counts, appearances, who they spoke with, longest exchange | Collection and curiosity: a person is more interesting than a chapter | Reading becomes meeting people. The data itself is a reason to open the app |
| **Reactions and notes on whole turns** | Saves the complete thought with its speaker and context attached, not a decontextualised clause | Memory that stays true to the text. A personal record that is re-readable |
| **The You tab** — heatmap, rings, streak, word mix by colour, voices met, next voices, thin eras | Reflection with content: it says something true about *how* you read, not just how much | Self-knowledge instead of gamification. It can name what you've been avoiding |
| **Plans and custom plans** | Catalogue for structure; custom plans for curiosity — any stories, whole books, every story one voice appears in | The reader's own question can become the plan. Structure without a straitjacket |
| **Streak on the local calendar; pause and resume** | Honest accounting on the device's own day; no penalty framing | Trust. A missed fortnight ends a streak, not a practice |
| **Free, offline, local-only, no account** | Removes every barrier between hearing about it and reading: no signup, no signal, no cost, no procurement | Anyone can act on a recommendation immediately. Schools and churches can adopt without a review |

**Supporting cast of features worth their own moments:** search scoped to voices, books, stories and
references across all 66 books, landing on the actual verse · share one speech bubble as an image or a
citation · New Living Translation, chosen because it reads well aloud · dark mode and adjustable text size
throughout · optional daily reminders scheduled on the device · English and French including all questions.

## 6 · How the design is built to create the impact

This is the section that answers "why is it *better*" without ever mentioning anyone else. Ten decisions,
each with the impact it was engineered to produce. Most of them are *removals*.

**1 · Attribution is the substrate, not a feature.**
The Bible data itself is attributed word by word to 773 sources. Everything visible — colours, Cast, the
word mix in You, the conversation graph — is a view onto that one asset. This is why the product cannot be
imitated with a theme: the work is in the data, and it was done once, exhaustively, across 747,039 words.

**2 · The layout makes a claim, deliberately.**
Narration and divine speech read down one side; named principals and everyone else read down the other. The
Bible is laid out as a conversation between God and humanity, and the reader absorbs that structure before
reading a word. This is a theological claim made by typography, and it was made on purpose.

**3 · Stories, not chapters — because completion is the engine.**
Chapter divisions are a thirteenth-century navigation aid. Stories are units of meaning: 2,047 words on
average, an arc, an ending. Completion is what brings someone back tomorrow, and a chapter rarely completes
anything.

**4 · The coordination machinery was deleted.**
1.x had QR codes, a host/join handshake and role-selection screens for group reading. All of it is gone.
The entire coordination protocol is now one sentence taught once in onboarding: *open the same story and
take a colour.* Group reading works better because there is nothing to work. **This deletion is the single
best story in the 2.0 release** — a feature was removed and the behaviour it existed to enable got easier.

**5 · Onboarding shows the real thing, in under 45 seconds.**
Four screens, never five. No illustrations, no icons standing in for Scripture: screen two renders an actual
passage with actual bubbles. Nothing is requested — no account, no email, no permissions. The product is its
own explanation, and the first real screen feels like a continuation rather than a change.

**6 · Ten divisions put the whole Bible on one screen.**
Collapsed, the canon is ten rows. Expanded, a division bows open to hold its stories. With scoped search
alongside it, the 365-item scroll problem disappears without a menu system reappearing.

**7 · One continuous line is the spine of the entire app.**
The same thread is the story list, the gutter in the reader, and the year in the You tab. One motif carries
navigation, reading and reflection, so moving between them never feels like moving between apps.

**8 · Light mode is designed first; dark mode is designed, not inverted.**
Both themes ship from one token set. The four source colours are re-chosen for the dark ground rather than
flipped, because the colours are the product and they have to hold in both.

**9 · The conversation graph turns statistics into curiosity.**
Precomputed at build time, with narration excluded from adjacency — otherwise the Narrator swamps
everything. It yields the facts that make Cast worth opening: Jesus speaks most often with the disciples,
Simon Peter and the crowd; Abraham's longest exchange is eight turns with God, in Sodom & Gomorrah; 559 of
the 773 voices speak in exactly one story. That last one is the whole product in a single statistic.

**10 · Nothing leaves the device.**
Progress, reactions, notes and plans live in SQLite on the phone. No account, no sync, no profile. This is a
product decision with a cost — no backup, no cross-device — taken because it makes the app usable in a
school, a prison, a camp, or a home that would not consent to an account. Say it plainly, and never
overstate it: crash diagnostics go to Sentry, and that is the only thing that leaves.

**Check before publishing:**

- **773 vs 774 voices.** The design document counts 774 (a union in which seven names are filed under two

  people stop believing.
- **"Fifteen to twenty minutes per story."** The data says a median of 10 minutes read silently. Read aloud
  with pauses, fifteen is fair; twenty is not. Recommend standardising on *"about fifteen minutes read out

**Never claim:** download, user or church numbers we can't cite; that the app contains the complete text of
the Bible (it is the biblical narrative in 365 stories); that nothing at all is transmitted (crash
diagnostics are).

## 9 · Go to market

**Owned first.** The store listings, the screenshots (one set per audience) and a landing page are the
whole foundation, and the landing page is the piece that doesn't exist yet — it is the single largest
constraint on discovery, since app-store metadata alone is nearly invisible to web search.

**Channels, in order of expected return:**

1. **Direct to leaders** — youth pastors, chaplains, small-group coordinators, homeschool networks. One
   page: what happens in the room, how long, what prep (none), what it costs (nothing). This audience
   redistributes; a single chaplain is thirty families.
2. **Short video** — the screen is the asset. Silent captioned recordings for feed, real audio for Reels
   and TikTok.
3. **Schools and camps** — the 180-story school-year plan and the school question set are purpose-built,
   and "free, no accounts, offline" clears procurement in a way almost nothing else does.
4. **French-language market** — full parity including questions, and almost nobody is competing for
   attention there. Do not treat French as a translation; run it as its own channel.
5. **Word of mouth at the table** — the share-a-bubble feature is the only viral loop in the product.
   Make it visible in onboarding and in one post a month.

**Asset list to commission or make, in order:**
the 20-second attributed-scroll video · four screenshot sets · a landing page · a one-page leader's sheet
(PDF, printable) · a family reel · a small-group reel · the Voice of the Week template · a French set of all
of the above.

**Sequence.** Weeks 1–4 as in the playbook (launch, together, Cast, plans). Weeks 5–8: leader outreach and
the landing page. Weeks 9–12: school-year push timed to term start, and the French channel.

## 10 · Measurement

**North star:** stories completed per active reader per week. It is the only number that tells you whether
the *whole story* promise and the *together* promise are both landing.

| Question | Signal |
| --- | --- |
| Does the pitch convert? | Installs by source; first story completed within 24 hours |
| Does it stick? | Day-2 and day-7 return; stories per reader per week; streak length distribution |
| Is together happening? | Share-a-bubble events; Talk about it opens; multiple completions in one session window |
| Which audience is real? | Plan mix — school-year vs family vs mini challenges — as a proxy for segment |
| Is the funnel honest? | Onboarding completion rate, and the drop between screen 3 and first story |

Note the constraint honestly: because nothing leaves the device, segment insight comes from store analytics,
plan-mix inference and talking to people — not from user-level tracking. That is the trade we chose, and it
is also a marketing asset.

## 11 · Risks and guardrails

- **The comparison table in `APP_DESCRIPTION.md`** ("Typical Bible app vs SourceView Together") contradicts
  the no-comparison rule and is the most quotable thing in the repo. Replace it.
- **Ninety principals, 674 supporting.** Sarah, Hagar, Hannah, Naomi, Rahab, Miriam and Mary Magdalene are
  currently filed as supporting voices — the same colour as "30 Men of Timnah". A *Women of the Bible*
  campaign that leads with the colour system will draw that comparison in public. This is a data edit, not a
  design change; make it before the campaign, or lead that campaign on stories rather than on colour.
- **Version numbers that disagree.** `app.json` on `main` still reads 1.3.0 / iOS build 23 / Android 22 while
  2.0.0 (26) is in review. Store copy, `APP_DESCRIPTION.md` and the repo should agree before anyone quotes
  them.
- **Privacy claims must stay exact.** "Nothing you do in it leaves your phone" is true of reading data and
  false of crash diagnostics. Use the precise form in any written material: progress, reactions and notes
  stay on the device; anonymous crash reports are sent.
- **Don't over-promise the together story to solo readers.** Most people will use it alone. Every audience's
  first ask must work for one person with one phone.
- **Free forever is a promise.** Once it's in the copy, changing it costs more than the revenue would.
  Decide deliberately.
