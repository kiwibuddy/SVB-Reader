## Part 1 — Summary of the review

Verdict: keep 90–95% of the plan. Strengths: the whole biblical story of promise and
fulfilment; starting with Revelation (Advent looks to Christ's return); complete stories
rather than isolated verses; the O Antiphons as an ecumenical bridge; a good mix of genres.

Theological principle: show how a passage points towards Jesus without implying every
original author directly predicted it. Distinguish direct prophecy, pattern, covenant
promise, typology and later New Testament application.

Story notes: Genesis 3:15 "traditionally" the first glimpse of the gospel; Genesis 22 a
"picture" of God's provision, not an explicit prediction; Isaiah 7:14 explain the Ahaz setting
and Matthew's application; add Isaiah 9:7; Isaiah's servant is sometimes Israel, sometimes an
individual fulfilling Israel's calling; soften "most quoted" for Isaiah 53; Jeremiah 29:11 is
to exiles; Ruth is a Moabite; Abraham's blessing reaches all nations; Philippians is all
narrator (weaker for four readers); Zechariah 9–14 needs guidance; Revelation 20:4 opens with
the contested millennium, so focus readers on chapters 21–22.

Traditions: evangelical and Anglican very positive; Catholic positive (different first-Sunday
readings, wider canon; O Wisdom draws on Sirach and Wisdom); Orthodox supplementary. Say the
plan is "informed by" the Revised Common Lectionary and complements church worship. Hope,
Peace, Joy, Love are this plan's themes, not a universal sequence.

Ideas adopted by the owner: show the O Antiphon prayers on Days 19–25; Advent questions; a
daily introduction with a key verse; honest reading time; the "God with us" thread
("God promised. His people waited. Jesus came. And we still wait for the day when he makes
all things new."). Not adopted: splitting Luke 1–2, re-segmenting Revelation, story changes
in 2026, a "three movements" structure.

## Part 2 — Draft daily introductions (ChatGPT)

**Week 1 · Hope** — God's promise, from the first pages of Scripture to Isaiah's Immanuel.

**Day 1 · The Holy City is Coming · Revelation 20:4–22:21** — The Bible's story ends with a promise: Jesus will come again, evil will be defeated, and God will make all things new. Revelation gives us a glimpse of a world without death, sorrow or pain. As Advent begins, we look beyond the celebration of Jesus' first coming to the day when he will return. The final words of the Bible are a prayer we can make our own: "Come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20).

**Day 2 · People Sin · Genesis 3:1–5:32** — The story of Christmas begins long before Bethlehem. In the garden of Eden, humanity turns away from God, bringing sin, suffering and death into the world. Yet even here, God gives a promise. In Genesis 3:15, he speaks of an offspring of the woman who will strike the serpent's head. Christians have traditionally seen this as the first glimpse of God's plan to defeat evil through Jesus. This is why the world needs a Rescuer.

**Day 3 · God Makes a Covenant with Abraham · Genesis 15:1–18:15** — God makes an extraordinary promise to Abraham: his descendants will be as numerous as the stars, and through his family all the nations of the earth will be blessed. Abraham cannot yet see how this promise will be fulfilled, but he chooses to trust God. Jesus comes from Abraham's family line, and through him God's blessing reaches the whole world. As we read, look for a promise that is much bigger than one family or one generation. *(Fix: use 17:4; "all nations blessed" is Day 4.)*

**Day 4 · Abraham's Faith Tested · Genesis 20:1–23:20** — God asks Abraham to offer his beloved son Isaac, the very child through whom his promises were to continue. At the last moment, God provides a ram in Isaac's place. Abraham declares, "God will provide" (Genesis 22:8). Christians have long seen this story as a picture of God's provision of a sacrifice, pointing forward to Jesus. As we approach Christmas, this story reminds us that the child born in Bethlehem came to give his life for the world.

**Day 5 · A Surprising Love Story · Ruth 1:1–4:22** — Ruth is a young widow from Moab who chooses to remain with her mother-in-law Naomi and trust in the God of Israel. Through her faithfulness, Ruth becomes part of the family of Boaz, and eventually the great-grandmother of King David. Her name also appears in the family line of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). A woman from another nation becomes part of God's unfolding story of redemption. This reminds us that God's promises reach far beyond the people we might expect.

**Day 6 · God Rejects Saul & Samuel Anoints David · 1 Samuel 15:1–16:23** — Israel needs a king, but its first king, Saul, has failed to follow God. Samuel is sent to Bethlehem to anoint one of Jesse's sons. The one chosen is David, the youngest, a shepherd overlooked by others. God's promise of a future King begins to take shape through David's family. Centuries later, Jesus will be known as the Son of David. As we read, notice how God looks beyond outward appearances and chooses according to the heart.

**Day 7 · Isaiah Volunteers; God Promises Emmanuel · Isaiah 6:1–9:7** — Isaiah receives a vision of God's holiness and is called to speak to a troubled nation. In the middle of political uncertainty, God gives a sign: a child will be born and called Immanuel, meaning "God is with us" (Isaiah 7:14). Isaiah also looks forward to a child who will reign on David's throne as the Prince of Peace (Isaiah 9:6–7). These promises become central to the Christian understanding of Jesus, whose birth we prepare to celebrate.

**Week 2 · Peace** — The prophets comfort God's people and point to the Prince of Peace.

**Day 8 · The Gospel Proclaimed; God & Idols Contrasted · Isaiah 40:1–41:29** — God speaks words of comfort to his people, who have experienced suffering and exile. "Comfort, comfort my people" (Isaiah 40:1) begins a message of hope and restoration. Isaiah also describes a voice calling in the wilderness to prepare the way for the Lord (Isaiah 40:3). All four Gospels connect this promise with John the Baptist, who prepares the way for Jesus. As we begin the second week of Advent, consider what it means to prepare our hearts for the coming King.

**Day 9 · No God like Our God · Micah 4:9–7:20** — Micah speaks to a troubled people, warning of judgement but also promising restoration. In the middle of these promises comes a remarkable announcement: a ruler will come from Bethlehem, a small and seemingly insignificant town (Micah 5:2). When Jesus is born, this is the passage the religious leaders point to when Herod asks where the Messiah will be born (Matthew 2:5–6). God's promised King comes from an unexpected place, bringing peace to his people.

**Day 10 · First Servant Song; God & Idols Contrasted · Isaiah 42:1–44:23** — Isaiah introduces God's chosen servant, who will bring justice to the nations. Unlike a ruler who uses force, this servant will be gentle with the vulnerable: "He will not crush the weakest reed or put out a flickering candle" (Isaiah 42:3). The Gospel of Matthew applies this passage to Jesus, showing how he brings God's justice with compassion and humility. As we journey towards Christmas, look at the kind of King God promises to send.

**Day 11 · Two Servant Songs · Isaiah 49:1–52:12** — God's servant is called to bring his salvation not only to Israel but to the ends of the earth. Isaiah celebrates the messenger who announces good news: "How beautiful on the mountains are the feet of the messenger who brings good news" (Isaiah 52:7). The apostle Paul later uses this passage when describing those who proclaim the gospel (Romans 10:15). God's promised salvation is not for one nation alone. Through Jesus, good news and peace are announced to the whole world.

**Day 12 · A Final Servant Song: God's Way of Salvation · Isaiah 52:13–57:21** — Isaiah describes a servant who suffers on behalf of others. He is rejected, wounded and crushed, yet through his suffering many will find healing and forgiveness. "He was pierced for our rebellion, crushed for our sins" (Isaiah 53:5). Christians have long recognised this passage as one of the most significant Old Testament pictures of Jesus' suffering and death. Advent takes us towards the manger, but the story of the child born in Bethlehem also points towards the cross.

**Day 13 · Letter to Exiles in Babylon: A New Covenant Promised · Jeremiah 29:1–31:40** — God's people are living in exile, far from their homeland, and wondering whether their future is lost. Through Jeremiah, God promises that their story is not over. His words about a future and a hope (Jeremiah 29:11) are spoken to a people who must wait and remain faithful. Jeremiah also looks ahead to a new covenant, when God's instructions will be written on people's hearts (Jeremiah 31:31–33). This promise finds its fulfilment in the new covenant Jesus establishes.

**Day 14 · The Good Shepherd Restores His People · Ezekiel 34:1–36:38** — God's people have been scattered, and their leaders have failed to care for them. Through Ezekiel, God promises: "I myself will search and find my sheep" (Ezekiel 34:11). He also promises to raise up a shepherd from David's family to care for his people. Jesus later describes himself as the Good Shepherd who knows his sheep and lays down his life for them (John 10). As Christmas approaches, we see God's promise to come close and care for his people.

**Week 3 · Joy** — Rejoice, the Lord is near. From 17 December, the ancient O Antiphons lead us towards Christmas.

**Day 15 · Preparing for God's Reign & Salvation · Isaiah 58:1–62:12** — Isaiah challenges God's people to move beyond outward religious activity and pursue genuine justice, mercy and compassion. He then announces the coming of God's salvation: good news for the poor, freedom for captives and comfort for the brokenhearted (Isaiah 61:1). Centuries later, Jesus reads these very words in the synagogue and declares that they are being fulfilled (Luke 4:16–21). The joy of Advent is not simply that a child is born, but that God's promised rescue is arriving.

**Day 16 · From Gloom to Ecstasy · Zephaniah 1:1–3:20** — Zephaniah begins with serious warnings about judgement, but his message ends with one of the most beautiful pictures of God's love in the Old Testament. God promises to live among his people, save them and rejoice over them with singing: "He will take delight in you with gladness" (Zephaniah 3:17). This is a surprising picture of joy. We often think about how we rejoice in God, but here we discover that God himself delights in his people.

**Day 17 · Book 4: God our King & Refuge · Psalms 90:1–100:5** — These psalms celebrate God as King, the one who has ruled throughout all generations and whose faithfulness never ends. Psalm 98 calls the whole earth to sing because the Lord is coming to judge the world with justice. Centuries later, Isaac Watts drew on this psalm when he wrote the beloved Christmas hymn "Joy to the World". As we approach Christmas, we join this ancient invitation to celebrate the coming of our King.

**Day 18 · Rejoice! · Philippians 1:1–4:23** — Paul writes this letter while in prison, yet its pages are filled with joy, encouragement and hope. He repeatedly calls the church to rejoice in the Lord, whatever their circumstances. "Always be full of joy in the Lord. I say it again—rejoice!" (Philippians 4:4). These words are also associated with Gaudete Sunday, the traditional Sunday of rejoicing during Advent. As we prepare for Christmas, Paul reminds us that lasting joy is found in Christ, not in perfect circumstances.

**Day 19 · God Came to Us · John 1:1–1:51** — John takes us further back than Bethlehem, to the very beginning of creation. He describes Jesus as the eternal Word, who was with God and through whom everything was made. Then comes the extraordinary announcement: "So the Word became human and made his home among us" (John 1:14). The ancient church's first O Antiphon calls Christ O Wisdom, recognising him as God's wisdom through whom all things were created. Today we begin these seven ancient prayers of expectation, looking towards the arrival of Emmanuel.

**Day 20 · Israelites Enslaved & Moses Called · Exodus 1:1–4:31** — God's people are suffering under slavery in Egypt, but God has not forgotten his promises. He hears their cries, sees their suffering and calls Moses to lead them to freedom. At the burning bush, God reveals his name: "I Am Who I Am" (Exodus 3:14). Today's ancient O Antiphon is O Adonai, recalling the Lord who revealed himself to Moses and led his people out of slavery. As we approach Christmas, we remember that God is the one who hears, comes near and rescues.

**Day 21 · Judah's Enemies Judged; Emmanuel's Coming Reign · Isaiah 9:8–12:6** — Isaiah describes a people facing darkness and uncertainty, but he also announces the hope of a new beginning: "Out of the stump of David's family will grow a shoot" (Isaiah 11:1). From what appears to be a dead and broken tree, God promises new life and a righteous King. The third O Antiphon calls Christ O Root of Jesse, celebrating the promised descendant of David who brings hope to the nations. God's promises can still bring life when everything seems lost.

**Week 4 · Love** — God keeps his promise: Immanuel, God with us.

**Day 22 · God Makes a Covenant with David · 2 Samuel 6:1–7:29** — King David wants to build a house for God, but God turns the conversation around and promises to build a lasting house for David: an enduring royal family and kingdom. God promises that David's throne will be established forever (2 Samuel 7:16). This becomes one of the foundations of Israel's hope for a coming Messiah. Today's O Antiphon is O Key of David, calling on Christ as the promised King who opens God's kingdom and whose reign will never end.

**Day 23 · Crucial Questions for Spiritual Leaders · Malachi 1:1–4:6** — Malachi speaks to a people who have returned from exile but have grown spiritually complacent. He challenges their worship and calls them back to faithfulness. Yet his message ends with a promise: "The Sun of Righteousness will rise with healing in his wings" (Malachi 4:2). The fifth O Antiphon calls Christ O Dayspring, the rising light who comes into a dark world. As Christmas draws near, we remember the hope of God's light and healing.

**Day 24 · The Coming Shepherd King · Zechariah 9:1–14:21** — Zechariah looks ahead to a coming King who will bring peace and reign over all the earth. Unlike the powerful rulers of the world, this King comes humbly, riding on a donkey (Zechariah 9:9). The Gospels later connect this prophecy to Jesus' entry into Jerusalem. Today's O Antiphon calls Christ O King of the Nations, recognising that his kingdom is not limited to one people or place. His coming brings hope to the whole world.

**Day 25 · The Kingdom is Inaugurated · Matthew 1:1–4:25** — Matthew begins by tracing Jesus' family line through Abraham and King David, showing how God's promises lead to this moment. He then tells of Jesus' birth, the visit of the wise men and the beginning of his public ministry. An angel explains that Jesus will be called Immanuel, meaning "God is with us" (Matthew 1:23). The final O Antiphon is O Emmanuel, the culmination of seven ancient prayers asking Christ to come. Tomorrow we celebrate the arrival of the promised King.

**Day 26 · Two Miraculous Births · Luke 1:1–2:40** — The long-awaited moment has arrived. Luke tells of the angel's announcements to Zechariah and Mary, the births of John and Jesus, the shepherds who hear the angels' message, and the faithful Simeon and Anna who recognise the child in the Temple. Mary, Elizabeth, the shepherds and others respond with wonder, praise and joy. "The Savior—yes, the Messiah, the Lord—has been born today in Bethlehem" (Luke 2:11). Today we celebrate God's promise fulfilled: Jesus, God with us.

## Part 3 — Draft daily questions and closing thoughts (ChatGPT)

Pattern: Notice → Connect → Reflect → Respond. Suggested instruction above the questions:
"Take your time with these questions. There is no need to rush or answer every question.
Listen to one another, share honestly, and look for one way to put what you have read into practice."

**Day 1 · Revelation 20:4–22:21**
1. What details stand out to you in John's picture of the new heaven and new earth? Which part would you most want to experience?
2. What does this passage teach us about what God is ultimately planning for his creation?
3. Where do you see brokenness, sadness or injustice in the world around you? How does God's promise of a renewed world speak into those things?
4. How could you bring a little taste of God's coming kingdom into your home, school, workplace or community this week?
Closing: Christian hope is not pretending that everything is good now. It is trusting that God will make all things new. As we begin Advent, we join the final prayer of the Bible: "Come, Lord Jesus!"

**Day 2 · Genesis 3:1–5:32**
1. What do Adam and Eve's choices reveal about temptation, trust and the desire to do things our own way?
2. In Genesis 3:15, God speaks of a future victory over evil. How does this help us understand why the world needs a Saviour?
3. Where do you see the effects of broken relationships, selfishness or sin in the world today? How do these things affect our own lives and relationships?
4. What is one area where you could choose to trust God and do what is right, even when another choice seems easier?
Closing: The Bible's story of hope begins in a garden where humanity turns away from God. But God does not abandon his creation. Advent reminds us that he came to rescue us and restore what was broken.

**Day 3 · Genesis 15:1–18:15**
1. What does Abraham's conversation with God reveal about his doubts, questions and faith?
2. Why is it significant that God's promise to Abraham includes blessing for all the families of the earth, not just his own family?
3. Is there something you are finding difficult to trust God with at the moment? What might Abraham's story teach you about waiting?
4. How could you become a source of blessing to someone outside your usual circle of family and friends this week?
Closing: God's promises are often bigger than we can see or understand at the time. Abraham's story reminds us that hope grows as we learn to trust the One who makes the promises.

2. Why do you think the Bible so often connects God's coming with both justice and healing?
3. Are there areas where our faith can become routine or disconnected from the way we live? What might help us become more wholehearted?
4. Who might be experiencing darkness, discouragement or uncertainty at the moment? How could you bring them encouragement or practical support?
Closing: The coming of Jesus is like the dawn breaking into darkness. As we wait for him, we are called to live with integrity, care for others and reflect his light in the world.

**Day 24 · Zechariah 9:1–14:21** (O King of the Nations)
1. What do you notice about the king Zechariah describes? Why is his humble arrival significant?
2. How does Jesus' way of being king differ from the ways people often use power and authority?
3. What can create division between people, communities or nations? How does the promise of Christ's kingdom challenge those divisions?
4. What is one step you could take towards peace or reconciliation with someone, even if it is a small one?
Closing: Jesus is the King who comes in humility and brings peace. His kingdom invites people from every nation and background to become part of one family under God's care.

**Day 25 · Matthew 1:1–4:25** (O Emmanuel)
1. What details in Matthew's account of Jesus' birth and early life stand out to you? What do they reveal about the kind of Saviour he is?
2. Why is it important that Matthew connects Jesus with the family lines of Abraham and David?
3. The Magi travelled a long way to worship Jesus. What might their response teach us about seeking and recognising God?
4. What could it look like to welcome Jesus more fully into your decisions, relationships and everyday life?
Closing: The promises we have followed through Advent now come together in Jesus. He is the promised King, the light of the nations and Emmanuel, God with us.

**Day 26 · Luke 1:1–2:40**
1. Which person or moment in these chapters stands out to you most? What does their response reveal about their faith?
2. What do Mary's song, the angels' announcement and Simeon's words teach us about the significance of Jesus' birth?
3. Why do you think God chose to announce Jesus' birth to ordinary shepherds as well as to people in positions of influence?
4. As Christmas approaches, what is one way you could make space for Jesus and share his love with someone around you?
Closing: The Advent journey ends where the Christmas story begins: with Jesus. The promises of God have taken on flesh. The Saviour has come, and the good news is for all people.

**End of the journey (finish screen source):** Over these 26 readings, we have followed a story that stretches from the first promise of rescue in Genesis to the birth of Jesus in the Gospels, and beyond to the promise that he will make all things new. We have seen that God's story is one of hope, peace, joy and love. We have encountered people who trusted, questioned, waited, failed, served and rejoiced. Through it all, God remained faithful to his promises. As you celebrate Christmas, consider how this story might continue in your own life. The story does not end at Christmas. Jesus has come, Jesus is with us, and Jesus will come again.
