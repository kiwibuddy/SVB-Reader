For schools, chapel & youth groups

Get thirty teenagers into a biblical text in one period.

SourceView Together turns Scripture into a script. Every line is coloured by who said it, so a passage can be read out loud in parts by students who have never volunteered to read anything.

The three things that make this hard

Nobody can tell who is talking

Quotation marks run for pages without attribution. A student who loses the thread in verse four has no way back in, and stops trying.

Chapters stop in the wrong place

Chapter and verse numbers were added centuries after the text was written. They cut stories in half, so a reading ends without an ending.

Reading aloud is socially expensive

Asking a teenager to “share” in a mixed-belief room is a risk they will decline. Asking them to read a part is not the same request.

Four colours, and nothing to explain

Every word in all 365 stories is attributed to the voice that spoke it, and inked in one of four colours. Students do not learn a system — they see it.

Narrator

Carries the story between the speeches.

God

Divine speech, including the words of Jesus.

Named people

Moses, Ruth, Peter, Paul — anyone the text names.

Everyone else

Crowds, soldiers, servants, unnamed voices.

Taking a colour

Before a reading, students tap a colour on the story’s call sheet. From then on their own turns are highlighted and everything else dims, so a student always knows when to speak without anyone cueing them.

  • Four readers covers most stories. Larger classes rotate weekly.
  • Nothing is shared between devices — no code to read out, nobody waiting to join, no lesson time lost to setup.
  • A student who takes “Everyone else” gets the crowds and the servants, which is often the largest part and the easiest to read.
  • Reading a part is a lower-risk request than sharing an opinion, which is why this works in a room where not everyone believes it.

Why the attribution matters

Over 700 people speak in the Bible. Once every one of them is named and coloured, a student can ask a question that ordinary Bibles make difficult: who is this person, where else do they appear, and who did they talk to? The app answers all three.

The twenty minutes after the reading

Every one of the 365 stories carries four discussion questions, and they are written three separate times — for a family, for a school room, and for a small group. They are not the same questions reworded. Switching audience is one tap.

The same story, three rooms — “The Flood”, Genesis 6–9

Family

“How can our family choose what is right?”

School

“When is it hardest for you to follow God at school?”

Small group

“How does this challenge our comfort with cultural norms that conflict with God’s ways?”

The shape of a school set

  1. What differences do you see between Noah and his world? Observation — what the text actually says
  2. Where do you see both justice and mercy in the story? Interpretation — what it means
  3. When is it hardest for you to follow God at school? Application to student life
  4. What small step of integrity will you practise this week? A concrete, measurable action

The four questions move from observation to action in the same order every time, so a student leader can facilitate without training.

Why this matters for a mixed room

School questions are written for middle and high school students in a room where not everyone shares the same belief. They ask what the text says and what a student notices — not what a student believes. A pupil can answer every one honestly without being asked to profess anything.

Marking what matters

Students can long-press any line to react to it or attach a note. Saved lines keep their voice, their colour and their place in the story, which makes them easy to return to when writing or revising.

Preparation required

None. Open the story, hand out the colours, read it, and the questions are at the bottom of the same screen.

Three ways schools are using it

Chapel

Four students read at the front while the room follows on their own devices. Rotate the colours each week so the same four are not always reading. Older students lead for younger year groups.

Small group

Six to eight students around a table, everyone with a colour, working the four questions afterwards. Upperclassmen facilitate for younger groups.

In class

Religious studies as the spine, with cross-curricular links to literature, history and ethics. The attribution makes the text easier to analyse as literature.

Your first month

  • Week 1 — pilot with four to eight students. Run one story from “God’s Story: The Good News”. You are testing the format, not the content.
  • Week 2 — rotate the colours. The students who read the narrator take a speaking part, and vice versa. This is where the confidence shift shows up.
  • Week 3 — run the questions properly. Give it the full twenty minutes rather than the last five, and let a student facilitate.
  • Week 4 — decide the spine. If it worked, start the 180-story school-year plan at the next term boundary so the year has a shape.

For your IT and safeguarding review

Free, with no in-app purchases to manage. No accounts and no sign-in — there is nothing to provision and no password to reset. No personal data leaves the device: reading progress, reactions and notes are stored locally, so there are no user records to hold or to request deletion of. The English Bible is bundled inside the app, so a lesson does not depend on the school network.

What it does not do

No audio

The students do the reading. That is the point of the format, but it means this is not a listen-along Bible.

No accounts, so no sync

Progress, notes and reactions stay on the device they were made on and do not follow a student to another one.

English only at present

French is planned but is not in the current release.

One translation

The New Living Translation, and it is the biblical text rather than a commentary or a study Bible.
